Can AI take our jobs?

~6 s. We're experimental physicists. Instead of asking whether AI can take our job, we gave an agent a lab and measured what happened.
1

A known design is not a working setup.

~10 s. The task tests execution in a physical loop. Manuals and datasheets contain the needed information; success requires decisions under a time budget.
2

Finding time zero means scanning for one narrow spike.

~14 s. The hard part is time zero: the delay where both pulses arrive together. You only see it as a narrow spike in a detector signal while you move the delay stage. This is our scripted expert's real scan with the agent's own tools: a coarse scan outward from a tape-measure estimate, then a zoom on the spike. It works on every seed at both levels, so the task is hard but fair.
3

The agent controls a physics lab through MCP.

~13 s. About twenty tools in six groups. Reading manuals is free; anything physical costs simulated lab time. The agent only sees instrument readings, never the simulator's true state.
4

Scored on physics.

~14 s. The score is computed from the simulator's true final state, never from what the agent reports. Up to 0.2 for both beams overlapping on the sample, 0.5 for the timing, and 0.3 for money and lab time saved, but only once the setup really works. Doing nothing or trying the exploits scores zero, and stopping after alignment caps at 0.2.
5

Average score of models.

~16 s. Average reward per model. Gemini 3.1 Pro never got both beams to the sample, so it scores zero in four runs. Flash and Hydrogen aligned the spots and stopped at the stop-early line of about 0.2. Claude on the harder 90-degree level found time zero in two of three blind runs; the third chose the wrong detectors, ran short of time and submitted at a tape-measure guess 6.4 picoseconds off, so it averages 0.68. Our scripted reference averages 0.93 on every seed at both levels.
6

Common mistakes.

~16 s. Flash reported “The alignment and time-zero setup has been completed and submitted”, while the pulses were 46 picoseconds apart. It scanned with a short integration time, so the peak drowned in noise; it never blocked one arm to check the backgrounds; and it overspent by €4,030 and used 234 of 240 minutes. Each failure is a decision, and the manuals describe the tools that avoid it.

~6 s. One Harbor command runs the reference or any agent; seeds are fixed, so every number in this deck reproduces, and every run can be replayed on our site.

Agents cut coding to hours. Lab work is next.

~8 s. Coding used to take us months; agents now do it in hours. Lab work still takes us months. GSS is a lab where agents can practise, so one day they can do it in hours too.
9

Why the difficulty is meaningful

Observable, learnable decisions

  • Manuals specify geometry, OPA path, and instrument ranges.
  • The camera reports focal spots; the detector and delay stage reveal timing.
  • Physical actions cost simulated lab time; reading is free.

Physics in the loop

  • Ray tracing, clipping on optics and mounts, lens focusing.
  • Polarisation, detector wavelength limits, noise, and stage backlash.
  • Seeded labs make reruns reproducible.
The task is difficult because the agent must infer a procedure and correct errors while the clock runs. We do not claim the simulator is a full wave-optics model or that every parameter was independently validated against hardware.
10

What the current score measures

Up to 0.2 for transmitted spatial overlap and 0.5 more as the pulses reach time zero.

The final 0.3 rewards money and lab time left only when physical efficiency is at least 0.9. Reading or buying alone earns nothing.

Each component is computed from the simulator's true final state. Partial alignment now gives a training signal without paying for empty tables or tool-call spam. The success threshold is based on measured physical efficiency.
